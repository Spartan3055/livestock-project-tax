Do 4-H and FFA Kids Pay Tax on Livestock Auction Money? (2026)

Educational only. Not tax advice.

Tax rules depend on each family's facts, and the earned-versus-unearned question for project income is not settled for every case. Check your numbers with a tax preparer or the IRS before you file.

Short answer: the money is income, but it is the net project income that matters, and many young sellers end up owing little or nothing. Here is what the IRS and extension sources actually say.

What the IRS says

IRS Publication 225, the Farmer's Tax Guide, has a paragraph called “4-H Club or FFA project.” It says any net income from sales or prizes related to the project may be subject to income tax, and to report the net income as “Other income” on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 8z, attaching a statement of gross income and expenses if necessary.

The same paragraph says the net income may not be subject to self-employment tax if the project is primarily for educational purposes and not for profit, and is completed under the rules and economic restrictions of the sponsoring 4-H or FFA organization. Such a project “is generally not considered a trade or business.” The National 4-H Headquarters fact sheet says the same thing.

Why the 1099 question is louder this year

Starting with payments made in 2026, the Form 1099-MISC threshold for other income is $2,000, up from $600. Some fairs are collecting W-9s so they can send a 1099-MISC when a youth's payments reach $2,000 for the year. The Michigan Farm Bureau FAQ points out that receiving a 1099-MISC does not automatically mean tax is owed. The reverse is also true: income under $2,000 is still income even when no 1099 is sent.

A worked example

Example numbers only. A market hog sells for $1,900 at auction with $250 in add-ons and $75 in premiums: $2,225 of project income. The pig cost $250, feed $640, vet $40, supplies and bedding $85, entry fees $35: $1,050. Net project income is $1,175.

If that is the child's only income and the child is a dependent, $1,175 is under the $1,350 unearned-income line for 2026, so the dependent income tests alone would not require a return. With a $2,000 1099-MISC, or with other income such as bank interest, the answer can change. That is why the calculator asks for everything.

Where it gets complicated

  • Earned or unearned. The Ohio State University Extension tax bulletin says project income reported as other income “most likely” is not earned income for the dependent standard deduction. “Most likely” is not certain. Treat it as a question for a preparer.
  • A business-like project. If the child raises animals year after year to make money, or has other farming, the project may belong on Schedule F with self-employment tax.
  • Large sales. Unearned income above $2,700 can bring in the kiddie tax (Form 8615).

Common questions

Is 4-H or FFA auction money taxable?

The IRS treats net income from sales or prizes related to a 4-H or FFA project as income that may be subject to income tax. IRS Publication 225 says to report the net income as other income on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 8z.

Do 4-H kids pay self-employment tax on a project?

Publication 225 says the net income may not be subject to self-employment tax if the project is primarily educational, not for profit, and done under the rules of the sponsoring 4-H or FFA organization. A project that is regular, recurring, and run for profit may be different. Ask a tax preparer.

Does a small sale mean no tax?

Often a dependent child with modest project income owes little or nothing, because expenses come off first and the 2026 dependent standard deduction is at least $1,350. That is not guaranteed. Run the numbers and check with a preparer.
